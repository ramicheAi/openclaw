# UNDERTOW (逆流 — Gyakuryū)

**A boy who fears water discovers he carries the ocean's rarest Current — and must swim to its bottom to bring his father home.**

---

**What it is.** UNDERTOW is a swimming battle-anime: real competitive swimming on the surface, an inherited mythic power system — Breath, Current, Fathom — underneath. **Why it's different.** No show has claimed water as its arena the way Haikyuu claimed volleyball or Baki claimed the body, though water is free spectacle, free danger, and free mythology in every culture on Earth. **Why now.** Swimming is the most universally practiced survival skill alive and a top Olympic draw, and it has never had a battle anime — this one already has its ending written, five seasons out.

## Format

24 × 22-minute episodes (Season 1 of a five-season arc). Shonen with dark undertows — One Piece warmth carrying Attack on Titan-grade secrets. **Built from:** One Piece (the declared dream, the found-family crew), Naruto (the outcast hero, the prodigy rival), Solo Leveling (a visible, chartable rank ladder), Attack on Titan (the hidden world, the buried-truth mystery), Baki (real freediving science as body-spectacle), Invincible (a father-shaped reckoning, none of the gore).

## The Hook — why swimming is a battle anime

- **Breath** — real freediving physiology (dive reflex, bradycardia). The floor; anyone can train it.
- **Current** — an inherited resonance with the ocean's great currents: the water agreeing with you, not magic.
- **Fathom** — a five-tier ranked depth ladder (Sunlit → Hadal) the audience charts like a stat sheet.
- **The result:** races read as duels. Panic is the villain, calm is the stat, and every win is paid for on screen.

## The Cast

- **Kai Isozaki (15)** — the boy who sank, terrified of water, carrying the deep's own current without knowing it.
- **Ren Kurose (16)** — the prodigy heir whose gift awakened shallow, and who can't forgive it.
- **Bo "Buoy" Fujii (16)** — no bloodline, monstrous trained Breath, proof the ladder is climbable on work alone.
- **Mirei Sanda (15)** — the tactician who reads a race like sheet music and gets pulled back into the lane.
- **Tsukiko "Luna" Amane (16)** — an unregistered free-diver who teaches Kai to breathe, torn between two worlds.
- **Coach Gouda (44)** — a broken Olympic champion who once had to put down his own drowned best friend.
- **Kemar "Riddim" Reid (17)** — champion of the unrecognized Freewater House, first to call Kai a brother.
- **Kaien Nakaru (38)** — the villain whose family the institutions let drown in silence, and who isn't wrong to be angry.

## Season One, Five Beats

1. A dying swim club conscripts the school's worst swimmer — who sinks in his first race, and doesn't drown.
2. A secret invitation leads him through a sealed sea-door into a drowned ruin: the pool was never the whole world.
3. A hidden institution reveals itself — ten bloodline Houses, a ranked ladder, and the truth that his father was a Warden, not just a swimmer.
4. Twilight qualifiers: his first current-clash, his first loss that matters, and proof that his Breath-only teammate belongs in the same water as bloodlines.
5. A rival faction unmasks the hidden world on live camera; the season ends on a Warden's ledger signed by Kai's father — three years after he was reported dead.

## The Endgame

Five seasons are mapped and the finale is already written, not promised. Kai's final descent goes past where his father waited; his answer to the series' villain isn't victory, it's mercy that costs him something concrete — he binds part of his own gift into the seal so no Warden ever holds it alone again, capping his own depth forever. He doesn't get his father back the way he asked for in episode one. He gets the truth, and gives away the one thing his father gave him — on purpose, in the light, to someone else. Every mystery seeded in episode one closes by the last frame.

## Why It Travels

Four-quadrant by design: kids get adventure and a power ladder they can track; adults get grief, faith, and institutions worth arguing about. The ten Houses are rooted in real global sea-mythology — Japan, Greece, West Africa, the Arctic, Polynesia, the North Atlantic, the Amazon, South Asia, East Asia — plus an eleventh, unrecognized Caribbean line fighting three centuries for a seat at the table. Every heritage appears the way it does in a real pool: everywhere, unannounced. And one lane no battle anime has had: the power system's buddy rules are the safety rules, and the show never shows a dangerous trick without on-screen consequence — a plausible, standing invitation to real swim-education and lifesaving partnerships.
