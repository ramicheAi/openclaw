# UNDERTOW Episode 1 Anchor Music Spotting Sheet

Spotting notes for the composer and music editor. Cues are numbered from the
episode (1Mx) and listed in picture order. All timings are against the first
draft teleplay and will be re-conformed to locked picture; every sync point
marked **HARD** must be measured from the cut, not assumed.

## The Episode in One Sentence of Score

Episode 1 is a single call and a single answer stretched across twenty two
minutes: the CALL is stated on the title card and the ANSWER does not land
until the camera reaches the roots at the bottom of the sea. Everything between
those two points is the Dive Reflex being broken and put back together.

## Direction: Cold Open

### 1M1 Deep Calls to Deep

This is the teaching cue for the entire series and it has one job before it has
any others: establish what an answer on time sounds like, so that every
deviation for five seasons reads. Open on the CALL alone over the sea's breath,
exactly as the teaser does under Kai sinking. Let the ANSWER come back **on
time** the first time, canonical, two bars behind and a fifteenth down: the
world is in order, a man is on an anchored boat at night. Then Daigo fixes on
something below, and the frame finds the LIGHT. On the light, the second answer
arrives **early**. Nothing else changes. Same instrument, same pitch, same
weight, two beats sooner, and the audience learns in eleven seconds what the
show will never explain to them. Daigo's line lands into the space the early
answer opened, which is why "…All right. I hear you" needs no scoring at all.
Tail out under the swinging rope lamp with the sub still decaying.

### 1M2 The Gunwale

Sixteen seconds and a child's version of the same five notes, up an octave,
struck glass with the mallet too light. It is not a theme for Kai. It is the
CALL played by someone too small to play it. Cut the music **HARD** on the jump.
Do not carry a single sample into the water. The Score Bible spends a whole
motif establishing that a hole in the music is an event in this show, so use the
licence on the first jump of the series and let a seven year old go over the
side in silence.

### 1M3 One Frame of the Eye

The longest cold-open cue and the first place the Fathom ladder does real work,
used as a continuous spectral map rather than as a rank stinger. Sinking from
Sunlit to Twilight across the descent: treble dies, space grows, image opens,
per the table. The CALL descends with the boy, once, unhurried. The father's
silhouette below gets the ANSWER register but only as a floor, no melody. Then
the light resolves into the EYE for one frame, and the ANSWER arrives **early**
again, on the frame, measured from the cut. This is the same statement as 1M1's
second answer at the same pitch and it must be recognisably the same event: the
deep reached for Daigo, and now it has noticed the boy. **Do not reach for
horror instrumentation here.** The Eye is not the antagonist and this show does
not own a monster sound. What makes it frightening is that the reply is warm and
comes too soon. Kai's scream costs him the cue: everything drops on the scream
except the sub, which survives one beat into black.

### 1M4 Bradycardia Age Seven

Pulse only. No melody, no harmony, no reverb tail worth the name. The Dive
Reflex figure exists in this cue purely as a slowing heartbeat, 72 easing
downward, planted so that when the same shape returns at 34 BPM in Act Two the
audience recognises it from a place they cannot name. Keep it Midnight dark. It
does not get to be warm yet.

### 1M5 Title Card

Fourteen seconds, the CALL alone, and the answer does not come. **This is not a
spend of the unanswered call.** The answer to this statement lands at 1M22, one
hundred and eighteen seconds shy of twenty minutes later, on the roots at the
bottom of the sea. Episode 1 is built as one enormous late answer, and the music
editor must protect the pitch relationship across the whole show: the card's
last note and 1M22's arrival are the same call and the same answer, and if the
episode is re-keyed anywhere in between, they both move together.

## Direction: Act One

### 1M6 Anchor

Thirty five seconds of nothing much, on purpose. Struck glass, close, narrow,
full attack, 12 kHz open: Sunlit brightness by the letter, because we are at
zero metres in a classroom and Rule 3 works upward as well as downward. **Strip
the low register out of the present-day surface world entirely.** No sub in any
Act One cue except where marked. The ANSWER lives between 87 and 147 Hz, and if
the audience does not hear that band for six minutes of school corridor, its
return under a name in 1M7 costs nothing to set up and pays for itself
instantly. On "Anchor's in this class" give the first two notes of the CALL and
stop. He has heard it for eight years; he does not need the whole tune.

### 1M7 The Kid Who Sits Out

Forty five seconds against the script's own montage length. Play the montage
lightly, almost tenderly, and save the whole cue for its last shot. On the
plaque reading **ISOZAKI D.**, bring in one note of the ANSWER register, the
first sub in the present day, and let it sit under the trophy case unresolved as
Kai walks past with his eyes on the floor. That note is the father. It recurs in
1M11, 1M20 and 1M24 at the same pitch and it is the most important piece of
motivic housekeeping in the episode.

### 1M8 Three People One Morning

The script asks for this explicitly, "intercut, wordless, scored to one piece of
music," so this is the episode's one song-shaped cue and its only chance to be
simply beautiful. Three figures, none of them a quoted motif, all built from the
theme's instruments so the sequence reads as the same world:

- **Bo** is breath. One held note that outlasts its phrase, badly and happily
  ornamented, matching a boy who sings off key and can hold a note absurdly long.
- **Mirei** is measure. A clipped metronomic figure that stops dead when she
  turns around and walks away, leaving the kickboard on the door. Restart it in
  1M19 and nowhere else.
- **Gouda** is the Fathom ladder applied to a person. Take the same struck glass
  and remove the top end: 4 kHz, long tail, wide. He is a man scored at Midnight
  in a room at zero metres, and that is the joke and the tragedy of the gold
  medal being used as a bottle opener.

Explicitly **no Riddim Break here.** Bo is the show's other body-clock character
and the temptation to give him the one-drop feel is real, but that motif belongs
to Kemar and the Freewater line and it does not exist until Episode 16.

### 1M9 Nobody Is Coming

Bo's held note from 1M8, alone, with nothing under it, ending before it wants
to. The hallway crowd is the arrangement.

## Direction: Act Two

### 1M10 Melon Bread

The most consequential decision in the episode. **Give the Dive Reflex to Bo
first.** Its settled, warm form is heard here for the first time, attached not
to physiology but to a big gentle kid offering bread on a stairwell landing, so
that when Kai finds the same sound on the pool floor forty seconds of screen
time later, the audience has already met it and already trusts it. That is
what makes the Act Two turn land emotionally rather than merely factually.

Two events inside the cue. On "Ask someone else," when Kai sees the pool through
the stairwell window and the almost-laugh dies, **cut the music dead** and let
the room carry it. Then bring the same figure back thinner and unaccompanied
under "I asked everyone else," so the second half of the cue is the first half
with a person removed from it.

### 1M11 You of All People

One note, the Isozaki pitch from 1M7, entering on the vice principal's line and
not before. Nothing under Gouda's feet coming off the desk. The picture is doing
the work and a stinger here would make it a reveal when it is a recognition.

### 1M12 One Length Any Stroke

The trap in this cue is dread. Rule 3 says depth dictates brightness and this is
an outdoor school pool in daylight, so the cue stays Sunlit: bright, close,
narrow, full attack, no lowpass. **Put the wrongness in the rhythm, not in the
spectrum.** State the CALL and take one beat out of it every bar, so by the time
Kai reaches the block the five note figure has been squeezed into four and a
half and the audience is anxious without a single dark sound having been used.
Mirei's "doggy paddle has precedent" plays over the top of it dry and deadpan;
do not score the joke.

### 1M13 Ninety One-Ten One-Thirty

Panic is a deformation of the Dive Reflex and never a theme of its own. Take the
identical figure from 1M10, speed it up and clip it, and **run the cue tempo off
the number on screen**: 90 at the top, 110 at the mid, 130 at the flash frame.
The audience does not need to be told they are hearing Bo's kind, safe sound
being wrecked; they will feel it and be unable to say why, which is the entire
design intent.

On the one flash frame of night-black water and the EYE, drop in a single frame
quoted verbatim from 1M3, same pitch, same sample if the sessions allow. It is
the cheapest and most effective device in the episode.

Cut **HARD** on the buzzer. The script gives the buzzer six beats of subjective
time; give it none of ours. The fall, the slap of limbs, the phone coming out
and the laughter from beyond the fence all play with no music at all. That is
the episode's second deliberate hole, and it is the reason the humiliation is
unbearable rather than merely staged.

### 1M14 Anchor Sinks

Thirty five seconds of the deformation at its worst, 150 BPM, clipped, with the
attacks stacking faster than the tails can clear. The laughter from above bends
into whale-song distortion in the sound design, so keep the music out of the 2
to 4 kHz range where that effect lives and let the two departments share the
frame. Kai's scream costs him his air; it should cost the cue its top octave.

### 1M15 Thirty-Four

The centrepiece, and the cue the whole first act was written to set up.

It is **one continuous cue with 1M14**, not a new piece of music, and the
transition must never feel like a cut to a different idea. The deformation
decelerates: 150, 140, and then the water settles and the figure begins finding
its true shape again. 90. 60. 40. Kai's clawed hands drift open on the same beat
the clipping comes off the transients. **34 BPM**, and by then the audience is
hearing, undeformed and unhurried, the exact sound Bo gave them on the
stairwell. The show's actual villain has been talked down in front of them
without a word of dialogue.

Depth handling. The venue is a four metre school pool; the frame goes from
chlorine blue to deep ocean cathedral. **Key the Fathom ladder to what the frame
says, not to what the venue is.** Sunlit into Twilight into Midnight across the
descent, following the lowpass, reverb and width columns. This is the one place
in the episode where Rule 3 and Rule 7 have to be read against the picture
rather than the geography, and the picture wins.

The low vast gentle tone, "like a whale, like a name," is the **ANSWER arriving
early**, third and last use in the episode, at the same pitch as 1M1 and 1M3.
The deep reached for Daigo on the boat, for Kai at seven, and for Kai now. One
idea, stated three times identically, so it codes as a single event across eight
years rather than as three separate moods.

What this cue must not do: the water paying attention, "dust motes and bubbles
all drifting subtly toward him, a pool-sized field of quiet gathered on one
boy," is the Undertow acting on water, and it is enormously tempting to reach
for Open Door's ascending sub glissando. **Do not.** Open Door is the only
motif in the kit that ascends in the low register, that exclusivity is the whole
reason Bring You Home will land five seasons out, and Kai has no technique, no
name for this and no idea what is happening to him. Score the gathering as
converging space instead: collapse the pre-delay and pull the early reflections
inward across the cue so the room shrinks onto him. Inward, not upward.

Also: **no Fathom rank stinger.** The ladder is not audible to the audience yet
and Kai is not ranked, not measured and not awakened on the record. The first
stinger in the series is Sunlit, in Episode 4, on the ladder's bottom rung. Ring
one here and Episode 4 has nothing to introduce.

### 1M16 Not Him

Intercut cue, and the problem it solves is that the two halves of the picture
are at different heart rates. Solve it with one piece of music heard two ways.
The deck material is the settled Dive Reflex from 1M15, but **lowpassed as
though heard from underneath**, so the deck's mounting terror is literally the
muffled version of Kai's peace. Mirei's voice rising as she calls the seconds
plays against it, unscored. Bo hits the water and the low end takes the impact.

Then Gouda. Strip everything to one held note under his face. He is the only
person on that deck who has seen this exact stillness before, and the correct
scoring choice is the cruellest one: he hears the safe sound, and it is the
worst thing that has ever happened to him. "…No. Not that. Not *him*" lands into
the tail.

## Direction: Act Three

### 1M17 Four Oh Seven

Hold music off through the phones, the murmurs and the stopwatch. Mirei staring
at 4:07 needs room tone, not a cue. Enter only after the PE teacher disperses
everyone and the laughter drains away, and then only the CALL, high and thin,
alone, over a soaked boy and an innocent blue pool looking at each other. No
answer. The audience is now two thirds of the way through an unanswered call
that started on the title card and they are beginning to feel the debt.

### 1M18 The Declaration

**No music.** Twenty five seconds of it.

This is the One Piece promise, said out loud, and it is the single largest
temptation in the episode to spend the resolution. Rule 2 forbids it: a resolved
answer is the most expensive thing in the score and this boy has, as of this
moment, sunk in front of his school and been filmed doing it. He has earned
nothing yet. Playing the answer at the call's own tempo here would spend the
series' most valuable asset on an unproven promise in the pilot.

So the dream is spoken dry, over deck ambience and dripping and one gull, and
the silence is not an absence but the loudest event in the act. Rule 6 exists
precisely so this can be done without reading as a dropout.

### 1M19 Three Faces

Enter **HARD** on Bo not laughing, and give each face its own established sound
in the order the script lists them:

- **Bo** gets the settled Dive Reflex, three notes, from 1M10.
- **Mirei** gets the metronomic figure from 1M8, restarted for the first time
  since she walked away from the natatorium. A scientist whose data just got
  interesting is a woman whose clock has started again.
- **Ren** takes everything away. On the crest of a wave swallowing a torii gate,
  the CALL goes up and **the answer refuses to come. A bare fifth instead.**

That treatment is Ren's, permanently, and it is already load bearing: Episode
4's cold open reuses it verbatim on House Kurose. Establish it here so the
Episode 4 quote is a callback rather than an introduction. The bare fifth is
now the sixth grammatical position in the [Score Bible](UNDERTOW-SCORE.md)'s
grammar table, so a composer working from the bible alone can find it under
that name.

Important accounting note: a bare fifth is a **refused** answer, not an absent
one. Episode 1 does not spend the no-answer card. Nothing in this episode does.

### 1M20 Forgive Me Daigo

The Isozaki note from 1M7 and 1M11, now underneath the name spoken aloud in the
present tense for the first time. Solo, no accompaniment, no movement.

The one trick in the cue: **do not stop it when Gouda closes the locker.** Let it
run one bar past the cut onto his face, unresolved, into the hold. He says he is
not going to train the boy; the cue does not believe him and neither does the
audience. That extra bar is the whole performance.

### 1M21 So Did Jonah

The only unambiguously warm cue in the episode, and it should feel like a room
with the heating on after twenty minutes of chlorine and salt water. Dive Reflex
at rest, undeformed, no deep register, Sunlit brightness by the letter, because
a kitchen at sea level is as shallow as this show ever gets and Rule 3 rewards
being obeyed literally here.

Under the grandmother, quote the CALL once in her register. She is the voice
that opened the episode with the psalm the theme is built from, and hearing her
speech shape and the theme's shape agree is the closest Episode 1 comes to
telling the audience where the tune came from. Keep it under her, never over.

Out before the last frames. Grace said over fish plays clean, so that 1M22's
entrance is a hard cut into open water rather than a crossfade.

### 1M22 Down

The biggest cue in the episode and the one Rule 7 fully licenses: the camera
leaves a lit kitchen window and does not stop until it reaches something sealed
at the bottom of the sea, and below Midnight the score may go as large as
anything in the genre.

Structure it as one continuous descent rather than as a series of rank events.
The whole Fathom table is executed as a single morph across the fall: lowpass
sweeping 12 kHz down to 800 Hz, reverb 1.2 s out to 7.0 s, image opening and
then closing again. **Follow the measured correlation curve, not a linear
widen.** The Score Bible is explicit that width peaks around Midnight at +0.81
and then returns toward mono at Abyssal and Hadal, because by then there is
almost nothing above the 120 Hz mono crossover left to be wide with. A music
editor reading "0.35 to 1.00" off the table as a straight line will get the
shape of the sea wrong.

The bark-glyphs spiralling down the trunk want motion, not melody. Save
everything for the roots. On the sealed geometric thing, **the ANSWER lands, on
time**, and this is the answer to the CALL stated on the title card twenty
minutes ago. It arrives at Hadal, which means it borrows Hadal's defining
property: **no attack transient.** It does not enter. It is simply already
there, and has been for twelve thousand years, and the audience only now got
deep enough to hear it.

It is the same tone the pool made. Same pitch, same instrument, different room.

**It does not resolve.** It is not at the call's own tempo. The world is in
order; the story is not finished.

### 1M23 End Title

Run 1M22 into the credits with no gap and no re-entry, so the descent and the
end title are one continuous fall. The cue runs the full length of the
existing 64 second concert statement and needs no edit; do not trim or extend
it to hit a different number.

## Direction: Post Credits

### 1M24 Awake

Almost nothing, and nothing melodic. The listening station is a cold dry room
full of brass and crystal that hums without power, so the acoustic is short and
the sound design owns most of the frequency range. Music contributes exactly one
thing: the ANSWER register, the Isozaki note, at the same pitch it had under the
trophy plaque in 1M7, under Gouda's recognition in 1M11, under Daigo's name in
1M20 and in the deep in 1M22. Five appearances, one pitch. By the technician's
line the audience has heard that note enough times to know it means the father
and the deep are the same fact, and they still have no idea why.

On "…It's awake," cut **HARD** mid-tail with the smash to black. No decay, no
reverb spill. The last sound of Episode 1 is an amputation.

Note for the reader tempted by this line: see the held-back list below. "It's
awake" is the exact trigger phrase for Current Wakes and Current Wakes is not in
this episode.

## What Episode 1 Deliberately Does Not Do

The pilot uses one of five motifs and two of five grammatical positions.
Everything below is withheld on purpose, with the intended payoff noted so the
series has somewhere to go.

### Motifs held back

### Grammatical positions held back

| Position | Status in Episode 1 |
|---|---|
| Answer **on time** | Spent twice. 1M1, to teach the baseline. 1M22, to close the episode's long arc. |
| Answer **early** | Spent three times, all on the same subject, deliberately identical: 1M1 on the light, 1M3 on the Eye, 1M15 on the pool floor. The deep reaching for an Isozaki is one idea, not three moods. |
| Answer in the **wrong key** | **Never used.** Held for Nakaru, Episode 20. |
| Answer at the **call's own tempo** | **Never used.** The pilot does not resolve. Recommended first in-series spend: **Episode 6, "First Length,"** on Kai's hand touching the wall, once, short, at the end of a dead-last fifty free he finished on his own power. ### Other things held

- **No Luna material.** She is absent by design until Episode 7 and has no
  motif, no instrument and no pre-echo here.
- **No horror instrumentation on the Eye.** Nothing detuned, nothing growling,
  no cluster, no low brass. The Score Bible's prohibition is written for Nakaru
  but the reasoning applies to the deep generally: this show does not own a
  monster sound, and the Eye is frightening because the reply is warm.
- **No low register anywhere in the present-day surface world** except the four
  Isozaki-note appearances. Withholding the ANSWER's 87 to 147 Hz band through
  Act One is what makes 1M7 free.

## Main Title and End Title Note

**Episode 1 carries no OP sequence.** The script goes cold open, title card, Act
One, and it should stay that way. Dropping a two minute opening song between a
seven year old drowning and a fifteen year old at a classroom window would cost
the episode its best cut. Run 1M5 as the entire main title: white on black, one
word, fourteen seconds, the CALL alone and unanswered. The full OP, per the
Series Bible brief of one continuous shot rising from Hadal black to a sunlit
pool ceiling, starts at **Episode 2**.

Two standing rules proposed for the OP when it arrives:

1. **The OP quotes the CALL in its first four bars.** Viewers who never
   consciously hear a cue still learn the tune, which is the point of writing a
   singable theme.
2. **The OP never contains the ANSWER.** The answer belongs to the show, not to
   the titles. It is only ever heard inside the story, which keeps every one of
   its five grammatical positions worth something.

**End title.** For Episode 1 only, do not use a separate ED song. 1M22 runs
into the credits with no gap and 1M23 continues it as the theme's 64 second
concert statement, treated down: lowpassed, wide-to-mono, still falling. The
episode should end inside its own score rather than beside it. A proper ED song
starts at Episode 2 alongside the OP.

**Post credits.** 1M24 sits after the end title, alone, with the cut mid-tail on
the smash to black. Confirm at the dub stage that no reverb from 1M23 is still
sounding when 1M24 begins; the two cues share a pitch and must not share a room.

## Notes for the Composer and the Redline

Three places where the script and the [Score Bible](UNDERTOW-SCORE.md) pulled
against each other, and the reading taken here.

1. **Rule 3 versus the Act Two turn.** The bible writes Rule 3 as geography, "a
   cue in deep water that is bright is wrong." Episode 1's largest cue happens
   in a four metre school pool whose frame explicitly turns from chlorine blue
   to deep ocean cathedral. Reading taken: **the Fathom ladder is keyed to the
   frame's depth, not the venue's**, so 1M15 travels to Midnight in a swimming
   pool. Recommend the bible add that sentence to Rule 3, because this will
   recur every time the show's subjective depth outruns its literal one.

2. **The post-credits line versus Current Wakes.** "…It's awake" is as direct a
   cue for Current Wakes as the series will ever hand a composer, and the motif
   is specified for exactly this event: a Current waking, not a Current being
   used. It is still wrong here. The motif's character is *welcomed*, over the
   relative major, quicker than the theme, and there is no welcome anywhere in
   that cold room. Playing it deformed to fit the scene would burn its first
   hearing on a version that misrepresents it. Held, and 1M24 uses the Isozaki
   note instead. Flagged for ratification, since a reasonable showrunner could
   overrule this.

3. **The teaser already spent the resolution.** The Score Bible states the
   answer at the call's own tempo is "used once in the teaser, on the title."
   That teaser is marketing and is out in the world, which means some part of
   the audience will arrive at Episode 1 having already heard the resolution
   once. Episode 1 does not spend it again and neither should Episodes 2 through
   5. Worth confirming the production is comfortable that the first in-story
   resolution, recommended for Episode 6, will read as an arrival rather than as
   a return.

One smaller item for the music editor: the Fathom width column is not monotonic
in practice. Correlation runs +0.99 at Sunlit, peaks in openness around +0.81 at
Midnight, and climbs back toward mono at Abyssal and Hadal, because the bass
mono-maker is doing its job and there is almost nothing above 120 Hz left at
those ranks. 1M22 traverses all five ranks in ninety five seconds and is the one
cue in the episode where reading the table as a straight line will audibly
misrepresent the design.
