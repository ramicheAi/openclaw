# UNDERTOW (逆流 — Gyakuryū, "Countercurrent")

**A shonen anime concept bible: the first great swimming battle-anime.**

Logline: *A boy who nearly drowned — and has feared water ever since — discovers he carries
the rarest of the ocean's ancient Currents, and must swim from the last lane of a school
pool to the deepest trench on Earth to find the father the sea took from him.*

Pitch in one sentence: **Baki's obsession with the human body + Solo Leveling's visible
progression + Naruto's outcast heart + One Piece's found-family adventure + Attack on
Titan's buried-lore mystery — in water.**

## 1. Why This Show, Why Now

Swimming is the most-practiced survival skill on Earth and one of the most-watched Olympic
sports — and it has **never had its battle anime**. `Free!` covered swimming as a
slice-of-life character drama; nothing has treated water the way Baki treats the human
body or the way Haikyuu treats volleyball: as an arena where mettle is forged and made
*visible*. That gap is the opportunity.

Water is also the single best canvas anime has:

- **It is beautiful.** Water animation is a flex every great studio wants (Ghibli's waves,
  Makoto Shinkai's rain, ufotable's water-breathing effects). The medium is the spectacle.
- **It is dangerous.** Unlike a volleyball court, the arena itself can kill you. Every race
  carries survival stakes for free — no contrivance needed.
- **It is mythic.** Every culture on Earth has sea gods, drowned cities, and flood myths.
  The lore writes itself from real human heritage — a genuinely global story engine.
- **It is universal.** Everyone has felt water hold them up and pull them down. The fear of
  drowning and the joy of floating are pre-cultural. A five-year-old and a fifty-year-old
  both *get it* in their bodies.

And thematically: **breath is mettle**. Swimming is the only sport where the fundamental
resource is the same one you use to stay calm, to not panic, to live. A show about breath
control is secretly a show about emotional regulation — which is exactly the lesson kids
and adults both need and never get preached at them.

## 2. Why the Reference Anime Succeed (Study)

Before building, understand the machines we're borrowing parts from.

### One Piece — the promise engine

- **Why people love it:** A promise made in episode 1 ("I'm going to be King of the
  Pirates") that the show keeps re-earning for decades. Found family — every crew member is
  a broken person who becomes whole *through the crew*. A world that keeps getting bigger,
  where every island is a new genre.
- **The lesson viewers absorb:** Dreams are declared out loud and defended. Your crew's
  dream is your dream. Kindness is not weakness.
- **What we take:** The declared dream (Kai says his out loud in episode 1), the
  crew-as-family structure, and the every-arc-a-new-world cadence (every Deep House
  territory is a new "island").

### Naruto — the acknowledgment engine

- **Why people love it:** An outcast the village wrote off, who wants one thing more than
  power: *to be seen*. The rival (Sasuke) gives the hero a mirror and the audience a second
  protagonist. Hard work vs. genius is fought out in the text itself (Lee vs. Neji vs.
  Naruto).
- **The lesson viewers absorb:** Being underestimated is fuel. Bonds are worth chasing even
  when the other person runs. Talent without work rots; work without talent still climbs.
- **What we take:** Kai as the mocked outcast ("Anchor," the boy who sank), the
  prodigy-rival two-hander with Ren, and the work-vs-bloodline argument built into the
  power system (Currents are inherited — but Fathoms are *earned*).

### Solo Leveling — the progression engine

- **Why people love it:** Progress is *visible, measurable, and frequent*. The weakest
  hunter becomes the strongest through a system the audience can track — every episode
  pays out a legible power-up. Pure satisfaction loop, brilliantly paced.
- **The lesson viewers absorb:** Compounding daily effort beats innate rank. The gap
  between who you are and who you could be is closed one grind at a time.
- **What we take:** The Fathom system — a numeric, ranked depth-progression ladder the
  audience can chart on a wall. Kai's rank at every point in the story is *known*, and
  every gain is shown being paid for.

### Attack on Titan — the mystery engine

- **Why people love it:** The world of episode 1 is a lie, and every reveal recontextualizes
  everything before it (the basement, the ocean, the truth of the titans). Enemies turn out
  to be tragedies. Stakes are real — the show will hurt you.
- **The lesson viewers absorb:** The monster was a person. Freedom has a price. Ask what's
  outside the walls — and be ready for the answer to implicate you.
- **What we take:** The hidden-world reveal structure (the swimming world of episode 1 is
  the "walls"; the Meridian and the Hadal Gate are what's outside), villains who were once
  swimmers "taken by the deep," and a father-mystery spine with a multi-season answer.

### Baki — the body engine

- **Why people love it:** Reverent, unhinged obsession with what the human body can do.
  Fights pause for anatomy lectures. Every opponent is a walking philosophy of strength.
  The father looms over everything as both goal and wound.
- **The lesson viewers absorb:** Strength is a discipline and an aesthetic. Respect every
  opponent's craft. The summit is lonely and the climb is the point.
- **What we take:** Cutaway anatomy explanations using *real freediving physiology*
  (mammalian dive reflex, bradycardia, blood shift, CO₂ tolerance — all real, all
  spectacular), opponents as philosophies of swimming, and a legendary father whose shadow
  is the story's gravity.

### The common denominators (why all five work)

1. **A legible want.** Every protagonist wants one thing the audience can state in a
   sentence.
2. **Earned escalation.** Power, scope, and stakes all grow, and the growth is *paid for*
   on screen.
3. **The opponent matters.** Great shows spend as much love on the rival/villain as the
   hero — every fight is two stories colliding.
4. **A truth under the world.** Something buried (One Piece's Void Century, AoT's basement,
   Naruto's Uchiha history) that makes rewatching richer.
5. **Emotion first, spectacle second.** The animation peaks land because a relationship is
   on the line, not just a fight.

## 3. The Synthesis: Design Pillars

Four decisions define UNDERTOW (alternatives logged in §14):

1. **Hybrid power system.** Real competitive swimming on the surface; a hidden mythic layer
   beneath. Races stay biomechanically real (Baki-real), but elite bloodlines awaken
   **Currents** — inherited resonances with the ocean's great currents (Solo
   Leveling-legible, Naruto-inheritable).
2. **Shonen with dark undertows.** One Piece/Naruto register — hopeful, funny, huge heart —
   but drowning is treated with real weight, loss is permanent, and the villains are
   AoT-grade tragedies. Kids get adventure; adults get the themes.
3. **Our world + the hidden deep.** Episode 1 is a normal Japanese high-school pool. By
   episode 12 the audience knows the world is bigger and stranger: a secret circuit, ten
   ancient Houses, a drowned civilization, and a sealed gate at the bottom of the sea.
4. **Underdog with a drowned secret.** The protagonist is the weakest swimmer in the
   school — because he is *terrified of water* — and carries the rarest Current of all.

## 4. World & Lore

### The surface world

Present-day Earth. Competitive swimming exists exactly as we know it: school clubs,
prefectural meets, nationals, the Olympics. This layer never becomes fake — surface
swimming remains a real, respected pursuit all series long, and several beloved characters
never learn the deep world exists.

### The First Drowning (deep lore)

Twelve thousand years ago, at the end of the last ice age, the seas rose and swallowed the
coastal civilization of **Nagi** — a culture that had learned to *listen* to the ocean's
great currents and move through water as easily as air. This is the show's unification of
real global flood myths: Atlantis, Mu, the Dreamtime floods, Noah, Manu. Every myth is a
memory of Nagi.

Nagi did not entirely die. Its ten great families reached different shores and seeded ten
**Deep Houses**, each folding into a local sea-tradition and surviving in plain sight for
twelve millennia:

| House | Seat | Rooted in | Signature |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kurose** | Japan | Watatsumi / ama divers | Vertical descent, patience |
| **Thalassa** | Greece | Poseidon cults | Open-water speed, storms |
| **Yemoja** | West Africa | Mami Wata / Yemoja | Healing, rip-current reading |
| **Sedna** | Arctic | Inuit sea-mother myth | Cold-water endurance |
| **Tangaroa** | Polynesia | Tangaroa / wayfinding | Navigation, wave-riding |
| **Ondine** | North Atlantic | Selkie / undine tales | Silence, stealth swimming |
| **Iara** | Amazon | Iara river myths | Freshwater, flooded forests |
| **Ganga** | South Asia | Ganga / Varuna | Current mastery, ritual |
| **Ryn** | East Asia coast | Dragon-king courts | Pressure resistance |
| **Isozaki** | Japan (open sea) | *Extinct — or so it's believed* | The Undertow |

(Names and rosters are v1 — the point is the structure: a global cast with real
mythological roots, One Piece scope with anthropological texture.)

### The Eleventh House — the Freewater Line (Jamaica & the Caribbean)

There is one line the Meridian's rolls have never recognized — and its story is one of
the show's most important. When Yemoja-line captives were carried across the Atlantic in
the Middle Passage, the tradition survived the crossing: it became **River Mumma** in
Jamaica, **Yemayá** in Cuba, **La Sirène** in Haiti — the same mother of waters wearing
new names on new islands. Out of that survival grew a pan-Caribbean line, led from
Jamaica, that calls itself **the Freewater House**: a House with no Pillar, because its
founders' Pillar was an ocean away — *so they rooted the House in each other instead.*

- **Seat:** the drowned city of **Port Royal, Jamaica** — a real city the sea took in
  1692, which the Freewater line quietly claimed as its hall: the only House seated in a
  drowned city young enough to remember being alive.
- **Signature:** *riddim swimming* — pace and stroke held to an internal drum line
  (nyabinghi-rooted), making Freewater swimmers the finest relay and open-water pack
  racers alive: a crew that swims to one shared beat moves like one body.
- **Philosophy:** *"The sea carried us here. The sea owes us the way back."* Swimming as
  freedom, memory, and joy — Freewater meets are the loudest, warmest events in the
  hidden world (sound-system culture, underwater).
- **Story engine:** the Meridian has refused the Freewater line formal House status for
  three centuries ("no Pillar, no House"). Their fight for recognition runs alongside
  Kai's unregistered-Isozaki mirror and Nakaru's institutional critique — the show's
  argument about who gets counted, made flesh. They are the first of the hidden world's
  powers to treat Kai as one of their own, because they know exactly what it is to be
  real and unrecognized at the same time.

(Built with tradition-keepers — Jamaican and wider Caribbean specifics are marked for
that collaboration like every House's are.)

### The Meridian

The Houses' hidden institution: part federation, part circuit, part priesthood. The
Meridian runs the deep world's law ("the surface must not know; the deep must not rise")
and its great competition — **the Fathom Games**, held every four years in a drowned
ruin of Nagi, its location rotating among the Houses. Events are not just races: depth
trials, relic retrievals from flooded temples, buddy-rescue gauntlets, current-line
navigation in open ocean. The winning House holds the **Wardenship** for the next cycle.

### The Hadal Gate

What the Wardenship guards. At the bottom of a hadal trench stands a sealed structure of
Nagi construction. The Houses' founding oath is to keep it shut. Almost no one alive knows
what is behind it — the truthful answer is seeded across the whole series (AoT basement
rules: the audience can theorize from episode 8, confirm nothing until season 3).

What the story eventually reveals, in stages: Nagi wasn't destroyed by the sea. Nagi
*chose* to sink — to seal something, and someone had to stay behind to hold the seal. The
Gate doesn't keep a monster out of the world. It keeps the ocean's grief in. And the
wardens who "drown" holding it — including, eight years ago, a man named **Daigo
Isozaki** — are not dead.

### The Drowned

Swimmers who went past their Fathom limit and were "taken by the deep" — changed by
prolonged exposure to the Gate's leakage into something between human and current:
beautiful, wrong, and unable to surface. The Houses hunt them quietly and call it mercy.
The series' first great moral rupture (mid-season 2) is the reveal that the Drowned are
still conscious. The monster was a person. It always is.

## 5. The Power System: Breath, Current, Fathom

Three layers, from universal to unique — every layer trainable, every layer legible.

### Layer 1 — Breath (everyone has it)

The foundation, and it is **real science played as power system**. UNDERTOW's equivalent
of Baki's anatomy lectures is freediving physiology, taught via gorgeous cutaway shots:

- **The mammalian dive reflex** — face contacts cold water, heart rate drops, the body
  remembers it was once a sea creature. (Real. Spectacular. Episode 3's whole lecture.)
- **Bradycardia and the "still heart"** — elite characters' heart rates on screen like
  power levels. Calm *is* the stat. Panic is the true enemy in every fight.
- **Blood shift, spleen contraction, CO₂ tolerance** — each a named, trainable "gate" in
  training arcs. Kids walk away knowing more real biology than a textbook teaches.

Breath training is where hard work lives. Anyone — bloodline or not — can build Breath,
and one main-cast character (Bo) becomes formidable on Breath alone. That's the Rock Lee
promise kept.

### Layer 2 — Current (the inherited gift)

Descendants of Nagi can awaken a **Current**: a resonance with one of the ocean's real
great currents (Kuroshio, Gulf Stream, Humboldt, Agulhas...). A Current is not shooting
water — it is *the water agreeing with you*. Awakened swimmers read flow lines like text,
ride micro-currents no instrument detects, and at high Fathoms bend near-body flow: a
wake that follows, a riptide summoned in a still pool, drag that just — isn't there.

Rules that keep it a sport and not a magic show:

1. **Currents only work in water.** On land, these are just very fit people. (Keeps the
   pool sacred; no power creep into generic superhero fights.)
2. **Currents amplify technique; they don't replace it.** A sloppy stroke with a strong
   Current loses to a perfect stroke with a weak one. (Work > talent stays true in-world.)
3. **Two Currents in one lane interfere.** This is the duel mechanic — "current clash" —
  and it's why awakened races feel like fights: your flow field and mine are arguing.

### Layer 3 — Fathom (the rank)

The measurable ladder — how deep your body-and-Current can go and *stay sovereign* (in
control, unpanicked, able to return). Tiers named after real ocean zones:

| Tier | Depth | In-world meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Sunlit** | 0–20m | Awakened novice. Most club swimmers cap here. |
| **Twilight** | 20–200m | Meridian licensing threshold. Fathom Games entrants. |
| **Midnight** | 200–1,000m | House champions. Perhaps forty people alive. |
| **Abyssal** | 1,000–6,000m | The ten House Heads. Living legends. |
| **Hadal** | 6,000m+ | Wardens only. To go Hadal is to risk becoming Drowned. |

Every training arc moves a number the audience can chart (Solo Leveling's satisfaction
loop), and every number is paid for in screen-time sweat (Baki's unspoken deal with the
audience). Kai's chart on the clubroom wall is a recurring shot for five seasons.

## 6. Characters

### Kai Isozaki (15) — the boy who sank

At seven, Kai went into a night sea after his father, open-water legend **Daigo Isozaki**,
and drowned — three minutes down before rescue. His father was never found. Kai came back
wrong in one specific way: he cannot put his face in water without his chest locking. At
his one school meet, he froze on the block, fell in, and sank in front of everyone.
They've called him **"Anchor"** ever since.

The hook: forced back into the pool at 15, Kai panics, sinks — *and doesn't drown*. Four
minutes on the bottom, calm as sleep, heart at thirty beats a minute. The water didn't
reject him. The water was *waiting*. Kai carries the extinct Isozaki Current — **the
Undertow**, the deep's own pull, the current that goes *down* — and the whole hidden world
has been looking for it for fifteen years.

- **Engine:** Naruto's acknowledgment-hunger + a wound-shaped gift: his power literally
  lives on the other side of his fear. Every Fathom he gains, he takes back from the sea
  that took his father.
- **Declared dream (ep 1, said out loud, One Piece rules):** *"I'm going to swim to the
  bottom of the sea and bring my dad home."* The school laughs. The audience doesn't.
- **Fighting style:** worst starter in the cast — flinchy, stiff, technique from a
  library book. Grows into a deep-and-vertical specialist: sinks when others can't,
  calms when others panic, wins long races on the turn and the final underwater.

### Ren Kurose (16) — the marlin

Heir of House Kurose, groomed since birth, the best age-group swimmer in the country under
his civilian name. Beautiful, cold, sprint-freestyle royalty. Ren was raised on one story:
that the Undertow line was the Houses' greatest shame and greatest loss — and meeting Kai,
an oblivious *nobody* carrying it, offends everything his childhood cost him.

- **Engine:** the Sasuke mirror — but inverted. Kai is bloodline-rich and training-poor;
  Ren is training-rich and (he secretly fears) resonance-poor: his Current awakened
  shallow, and House Kurose does not forgive shallow. His arc is learning that depth
  isn't inherited either.
- **Relationship:** rival → begrudging lane-mate → the series' co-protagonist. The
  Naruto/Sasuke engine with a healthier trajectory — he runs *toward* the fight, not away.

### The crew (found family, One Piece rules — each broken, each completed by the team)

- **Bo "Buoy" Fujii (16)** — enormous, gentle breaststroker; zero bloodline, monstrous
  Breath built hauling nets on his family's fishing boat. The heart of the crew and its
  Rock Lee: proof the ladder is climbable on work alone. Comic relief who is never the
  joke when it matters.
- **Mirei Sanda (15)** — backstroke tactician, data obsessive, reads a race like sheet
  music. Ex-swimmer's-kid who quit after burnout; joins as "manager," gets pulled back
  into the lane. Arc: from optimizing everything to trusting one instinct at the moment
  the numbers run out.
- **Tsukiko "Luna" Amane (16)** — free-diving girl from an ama-diver island, House-adjacent
  but unregistered; swims like the pool is a formality. Kai's guide into the deep world
  and his breath-teacher. Arc: choosing between the island's hidden old ways and the
  Meridian's institutions — both of which claim her.

### Coach Gouda "the Beached God" (44)

Olympic gold, then a Meridian champion, then the man who watched his best friend go past
Midnight and come back Drowned — and put him down himself. Now a rumpled high-school coach
who teaches biomechanics like scripture and drinks like he's trying to forget scripture.
Recognizes what Kai is in episode 2 and spends six episodes trying *not* to train him,
because he knows exactly where the deep road ends.

- **Function:** Baki's lecture-voice + the genre's damaged-mentor archetype played
  honestly. His arc is the adult lesson of the show: the courage to let someone you love
  attempt the thing that destroyed you.

### Daigo Isozaki — the Tide King (absent)

The greatest open-water swimmer who ever lived, publicly drowned eight years ago
(named Warden-designate seven years before that — dates per the lore codex's logged
continuity amendment).
Actually: the last Warden, holding the Hadal Gate from the inside. The father-shadow that
gives the series its Baki-gravity and its AoT-mystery — every season peels one layer off
what he knew, what he chose, and what it cost the family he left on the beach.

## 7. Antagonists

### Arc antagonists — the House champions

Baki structure: each major competition arc is headlined by a rival House's champion, and
each champion is a *philosophy of swimming* to be understood, not just beaten:

- **Nereus Thalassa** — storm-swimmer; believes races are won before the start, by fear.
- **Aput Sedna** — cold-water monk; swims Arctic leads barefoot; believes suffering is the
  only honest coach. (Season 2's "training arc as theology.")
- **Moana-Rangi Tangaroa** — wayfinder; never swims the shortest line, always the *right*
  one; the first opponent who beats Kai kindly, and the most beloved.

### The series villain — Kaien Nakaru, "the Second Tide"

Former Fathom Games champion of House Ryn. Eleven years ago, a tsunami erased his home
town — a tsunami the Meridian's deep-listeners detected forty minutes early and said
nothing about, because *the surface must not know*. His family drowned in the silence of
that oath. Nakaru went Hadal alone, survived past the Gate's threshold, and came back
half-Drowned and wholly certain: the Houses' secrecy is a crime with a body count, and
the only honest world is one where the sea's truth is loosed on everyone equally.

He is not wrong about the crime. He is wrong about the sentence. That's the AoT bar for a
villain: the audience should spend mid-series arguing about whether he has a point,
because he does — Kai's answer to him can't be "you're evil," it has to be *better*.

His faction — **the Second Tide** — recruits exactly whom you'd expect: washouts the
Houses discarded, families of the Drowned, surface swimmers who found the hidden world
and were memory-bound by Meridian law. Every grunt has a grievance the Houses earned.

## 8. Story Structure

### Season 1 (24 episodes) — "The Boy Who Sank"

- **Arc 1: The Anchor (eps 1–6).** School-club story, played straight and warm. Kai is
  conscripted into a dying swim club (they need a fourth or they're disbanded — sports
  anime rules, honored knowingly). The fear, the sinking, the four impossible minutes on
  the bottom. Gouda notices. Ends with Kai's first finished race: dead last, and the
  club screaming like he won gold. *The audience thinks they're watching a sports show.*
- **Arc 2: The Hidden Lane (eps 7–12).** Luna arrives; a Meridian scout arrives wearing a
  federation blazer; a "night meet" invitation that isn't from any federation. The reveal
  dive — Kai's first descent into a drowned Nagi ruin — is the season's signature
  sequence and the genre turn: *the pool was the walls; this is outside them.* Ren
  learns who Kai is. It goes badly.
- **Arc 3: Twilight Trials (eps 13–19).** Training arc (Breath gates, dive-reflex
  lectures, Fathom chart on the wall) into the Meridian qualifiers: five events, three
  House champions, Kai's first current-clash — and his first loss that matters, to
  Moana-Rangi, who then teaches him why. Bo qualifies on Breath alone; the deep world's
  bloodline snobbery meets its first counterexample.
- **Arc 4: The Second Tide (eps 20–24).** Nakaru's faction strikes the qualifiers — not
  to kill, to *unmask*: they breach the event to the surface world and force the Houses'
  cover-up machinery into the open where Kai can see it. Season ends on the double
  reveal: what the Meridian did to Nakaru's town — and a recovered Warden's ledger
  bearing a signature dated *three years after* Daigo Isozaki drowned.

### The macro arc (five seasons, sketched)

- **S2 — "Ten Houses":** the Fathom Games proper. Tournament season; the Drowned
  reveal breaks the crew's faith in the Meridian mid-tournament.
- **S3 — "The Drowned":** hunting the truth of the Drowned with Nakaru as shadow-ally;
  ends at the trench lip, with the truth of Nagi's choice and Daigo's voice on the
  hydrophone.
- **S4 — "The Second Drowning":** Nakaru moves to open the Gate; Houses fracture into
  camps (the AoT civil-conflict pivot, kept shonen-hopeful by the crew's line-holding).
- **S5 — "Hadal":** the descent. Kai goes where only Wardens go, past where his father
  waited, and answers the series' question — *what do you owe the thing that almost
  killed you and made you who you are?* The last stroke of the series is the first one
  his father taught him.

Every season keeps a real surface-swimming milestone alive in parallel (prefecturals →
nationals → Olympic trials) so the sports show never dissolves into the fantasy show —
the double life is a permanent engine, Spider-Man-style.

## 9. Race & Battle Design

The action grammar — what an UNDERTOW set-piece is made of:

1. **The start is a duel of stillness.** Heart-rate overlays on the blocks. The calmest
   swimmer wins the start; panic is the first villain of every race. (Mettle, made visual.)
2. **Current-clash.** Two awakened swimmers in adjacent lanes deform each other's flow —
   animated as interfering wake-patterns, read by commentators like a chess match. Won by
   technique and psychology, not wattage.
3. **The underwater is the sword-clash.** Post-turn dolphin-kick sequences are shot like
   duels: silence, bubbles, two bodies in the blue, heartbeat percussion. Baki-style
   cutaways name the real muscles and the real physics doing the work.
4. **Deep trials are dungeon crawls.** Meridian events in drowned ruins: relic retrieval,
   buddy-rescue gauntlets (you *cannot* clear them alone — teamwork mechanically
   enforced), current-line navigation. Solo Leveling's dungeon shape, no monsters
   required — pressure, cold, dark, and the clock are monster enough.
5. **Nobody drowns casually.** Drowning on screen is rare, quiet, and shot with
   documentary respect. When the show finally spends a death, it spends it at full
   price. (This is the AoT lesson about stakes *and* the water-safety ethic in one.)

## 10. Themes & Lessons (Kids and Adults)

The dual-register design — one show, two depths, no talking down:

| Thread | What a kid takes | What an adult takes |
|---|---|---|
| **Breath** | "Calm is a superpower you can train." | Emotional regulation as lifelong practice; panic, anxiety, and the body's override switches. |
| **Fear** | Courage means doing it scared. | Kai's arc is trauma recovery done honestly: fear never fully leaves; it becomes information. |
| **Work vs. talent** | Bo's climb: effort is a real ladder. | Inheritance (of gifts and of wounds) is real *and* is not destiny — the Naruto argument, resolved with more honesty. |
| **Crew** | Your team's dream is your dream. | Found family as the answer to institutional failure — the Houses fail everyone; the crew doesn't. |
| **The sea** | Respect the water; it is not your enemy, and it is not your friend. | Grief. The ocean is the show's image for everything that takes and gives without malice. |
| **Truth** | Secrets that protect the strong hurt the small. | Nakaru's question — when institutions lie for stability, what does an honest person owe the lie? |
| **Safety (explicit)** | Never swim alone; the buddy rules are *in the power system*. | The show's quiet public-service spine: real drowning-prevention practice modeled constantly and never mocked. |

The mettle thesis, stated once by Gouda and proven forever after: *"The water doesn't
care how strong you are. It asks one question, every time: can you stay yourself in it?
That's the whole sport. That's the whole life."*

## 11. Visual & Sound Design Language

### Water is a character

- **Calm water**: Ghibli-weight — heavy, glassy, holds light like a solid.
- **Race water**: hybrid 2D/FX — hand-drawn character animation over simulated flow
  fields, so current-clashes read as *drawn* violence, not particle soup.
- **Deep water**: the show's second world. Below Twilight, the palette leaves realism:
  bioluminescent Nagi ruins, marine-snow like inverted starfields, blacks that are blue
  if you stare. The deep should feel the way space films feel — sacred and indifferent.

### Palette by depth (the show's visual spine)

Sun-bleached chlorine cyan (school arcs) → green-gold open water → violet twilight →
bioluminescent midnight → Hadal, where the only colors are what characters bring with
them. A viewer can pause any frame and know the depth — and therefore the stakes — from
color alone.

### Character design

- Real swimmers' body diversity, not one hero physique: sprinters built like Ren, distance
  swimmers lean as wire, Bo's working-boat mass, Luna's compact diver frame. Baki's
  reverence for bodies, minus the single-template problem.
- **Surface kit**: real tech suits, caps, club colors — grounded, brand-real.
- **Deep kit**: each House's ancestral second-skin suit, patterned after its founding
  culture's real textile and tattoo traditions (researched, credited, consulted — not
  costume-shop pastiche). House sigils read underwater at distance, like heraldry.
- Awakened eyes take on their Current's color *only when submerged* — the transformation
  shot every episode earns its poster frame with.

### Sound

- The underwater mix is the show's signature: muffled world, amplified body — heartbeat,
  breath, blood. Silence used the way AoT uses its silences: before the worst moments.
- Each House carries a musical tradition (taiko for Kurose, Polynesian chant for Tangaroa,
  Inuit katajjaq for Sedna, nyabinghi drums and deep reggae bass for the Freewater line —
  whose races are *scored to their own riddim* on screen) — the score itself is the
  world's anthropology.
- OP brief: one continuous shot rising from Hadal black to a sunlit pool ceiling,
  five seasons of visual foreshadowing hidden in the ruins it passes.

## 12. Episode 1 Outline — "Anchor"

1. **Cold open (pre-title):** Night sea, eight years ago. A man swims into black water
   toward a light below. A seven-year-old follows. Three minutes of the most beautiful,
   most frightening water ever animated. Title card.
2. **Act 1:** Kai at 15 — the kid who sits out swim class, the "Anchor" jokes, the dying
   swim club's noticeboard plea. Establish the crew-to-be in their civilian misery: Bo
   hauling nets at dawn, Mirei quitting her federation club, coach Gouda asleep in the
   equipment room at a school that's forgotten who he was.
3. **Act 2:** The conscription (club needs a fourth by Friday or loses the pool). Kai's
   refusal; Bo's guileless ask — *"you don't have to swim fast, just swim with us"* —
   the found-family thesis in one line. The forced first practice. The panic. The fall.
4. **Act 3:** The sinking, played first as horror — then the turn: the noise stops, the
   fear stops, heart-rate on screen dropping 90… 60… 34, and Kai opens his eyes on the
   pool floor, *comfortable*, as something vast and gentle in the water pays attention.
   Four minutes. Gouda, on the deck, going pale for a reason no student understands.
5. **Button:** Pulled out, wrapped in a towel, humiliated — Kai looks at the water that
   has ruined his life twice, and says the dream out loud: *"I'm going to swim to the
   bottom of the sea and bring my dad home."* Laughter. One face not laughing: a
   transfer student in a Kurose-crest tracksuit, seeing exactly what just happened.
6. **Post-credits:** A hydrophone array, somewhere cold. A technician frowning at a
   waveform that hasn't appeared in fifteen years. Phone call. *"…It's awake."*

## 13. Title, Naming, and Franchise Notes

- **Primary title: UNDERTOW** — English one-word, globally pronounceable, literally the
  protagonist's power and thematically "the pull beneath the surface of everything."
  Japanese title **逆流 (Gyakuryū, "countercurrent")** — the underdog thesis in one word.
- Alternates considered: *FATHOM* (strong, colder), *DEEP KING* (Baki-flavored, less
  four-quadrant), *The Boy Who Sank* (novel/film-adaptation flavor; kept as Arc 1 title).
- Franchise surface: the Fathom ladder and House system are natively game-able (ranks,
  events, crews) and merch-able (House sigils/colors); the real-physiology angle gives
  the show an education/outreach lane (learn-to-swim and water-safety partnerships) that
  no battle anime has ever had. UNDERTOW can plausibly *teach kids to swim.*

## 14. Open Creative Decisions

Choices made for v1, with the forks preserved — any of these can be re-decided:

1. **Power system — chose: hybrid (real sport + mythic depths).**
   Alternatives: fully grounded Baki-style (no powers, pure physiology and will) or full
   fantasy water-world (swimming as the combat system of an invented world). The hybrid
   was chosen because it keeps both promised audiences: sports-anime hearts and
   battle-shonen scale.
2. **Tone — chose: shonen with dark undertows.** Alternatives: seinen AoT-register
   (children die, institutions win) or bright all-ages. Chosen register is the proven
   four-quadrant formula: One Piece's warmth carrying AoT-weight secrets.
3. **World — chose: our world + hidden deep.** Alternatives: pure real-world sports drama,
   or original water-planet. Chosen because the hidden-world reveal *is* the AoT engine,
   and because real-world swimming keeps the show's feet (and its water-safety mission)
   on the ground.
4. **Protagonist — chose: underdog with a drowned secret.** Alternatives: fallen prodigy
   (Baki-flavored return-to-summit) or dual-protagonist rival pair from ep 1. Chosen
   because the wound-is-the-gift structure gives the longest, truest emotional runway —
   and the rival pair emerges organically by mid-season anyway.
