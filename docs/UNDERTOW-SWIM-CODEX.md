## 1. Competitive swimming, correctly

### The four strokes — what actually generates propulsion

**Freestyle (front crawl).** Not a paddle-wheel pull. Propulsion comes from the *catch*:
the swimmer sets the forearm and hand vertical early in the underwater pull
("early vertical forearm," EVF) and presses back against still water using the forearm
and hand as a single flat surface, not a bent, sculling scoop. The pull is a diagonal,
S-ish path only insomuch as the body is rotating under a hand that's holding its place in
the water — modern technique minimizes the S and maximizes hold. Body roll (rotation
about the long axis) lets the strongest muscles (lats, not shoulders) drive the pull and
shortens the recovery arc. The flutter kick is mostly balance and rotation-timing at
distance pace; at sprint pace, a fast, tight 6-beat kick becomes a real, measurable
propulsive contributor.
*Dramatic use: the EVF "catch" is Kai's tell — Gouda spots the Undertow current not from
speed but from a catch that holds water no untrained 15-year-old should be able to hold.*

**Backstroke.** Mechanically freestyle's mirror — same rotation-driven pull, same
high-elbow catch, done supine. The hard part is real: no visual reference to the wall,
so backstrokers count strokes from the flags (5m from the wall) to time the turn blind.
*Dramatic use: Mirei's stroke — she "reads the pool blind" the way she reads a race with
numbers; backstroke becomes her signature because it's the stroke won by preparation, not
sight.*

**Breaststroke.** The whip kick (heels drawn to the glutes, feet turned out, then snapped
around and back together) is the single largest propulsive event in competitive
swimming — more powerful, stroke for stroke, than any pull in any stroke. The pull is a
short outsweep-insweep that sets up the kick and provides some drive of its own, followed
by a glide. Timing (pull-breathe-kick-glide) matters more than raw power; breaststroke
rewards patience and punishes anyone who rushes the glide.
*Dramatic use: Bo's stroke. Breaststroke is the "no bloodline needed" stroke in-world —
its power comes from technique and hip mobility, which is exactly why the Rock-Lee
character swims it.*

**Butterfly.** One continuous whip-like body wave (chest presses down and forward while
hips rise, in sequence, not a stiff-hipped push) drives two dolphin kicks per arm cycle,
timed with the pull. Recovery is simultaneous and entirely over the water — there is no
underwater recovery in fly, which is why it punishes poor timing worse than any other
stroke: get the wave wrong and you claw at the surface instead of riding it.
*Dramatic use: fly is the "current clash" stroke of choice in the show — its whole-body
undulation is the closest real technique gets to looking like a dragon's wake. House
Kurose's ancestral deep-water forms are fly-derived for exactly this reason — though Ren
himself races sprint freestyle on the surface (bible §6), which is its own beat: the heir
who won't swim his House's stroke.*

### Starts, breakouts, turns

**Starts.** From the block, swimmers use a track start (one foot forward, one back) far
more often now than the old parallel grab start — it loads the back leg for a faster,
flatter entry. Reaction time off the beep genuinely decides close 50s; a false start today
is a single-strike DQ (older multi-warning rules are gone).
*Dramatic use: the "duel of stillness" (bible §9) — heart-rate overlays on the blocks
literalize what real elite starters already do: control arousal so reaction time doesn't
spike from adrenaline overshoot.*

**Breakouts.** The transition from underwater streamline to the first surface stroke.
Timing it late (riding the underwater speed longer, since a body underwater is faster
than a body making surface waves) versus early (getting stroke rate going sooner) is a
real, coached, race-plan decision — not an accident.
*Dramatic use: an awakened swimmer's breakout is the "sheathing the blade" beat — the
moment Current-assisted speed has to convert back into legal, judged technique.*

**Turns.** Freestyle and backstroke use the flip (tumble) turn: a forward somersault into
a foot-plant on the wall, rotating from prone/supine into a streamlined push-off on the
back or side. Breaststroke and butterfly require a two-hand simultaneous touch, then an
open turn (tuck, rotate, plant, push). Missing a simultaneous two-hand touch, or touching
with one hand late, or touching with the shoulders not "generally on the breast" in fly,
is a real, common DQ.
*Dramatic use: the turn is the show's re-set beat mid-race — the one place a losing
swimmer can legally buy back tempo, and the one place a current-clash resets.*

**Underwater dolphin kick and the real 15m rule.** After the start and after every turn,
a swimmer may travel underwater in streamline using dolphin kick (or, for breaststroke, a
single restricted underwater pull-and-kick cycle — see below) but must break the surface
of the water by the 15-meter mark, for freestyle, backstroke, and butterfly. Breaststroke
has its own separate rule: after the start/turn, one arm pull all the way to the hip plus
one dolphin kick plus one breaststroke kick is permitted before the head must break the
surface — it isn't governed by the 15m mark the same way, because a breaststroker's
underwater phase is capped by stroke-cycle count, not distance. Underwater dolphin kick is
often faster than any surface stroke, which is exactly why the rule exists: cap the
underwater phase or every race becomes an underwater race.
*Dramatic use: this is UNDERTOW's single best-integrated rule-as-story-beat. The
underwater is where the show's power system is most dangerous and most honest — no
current-clash wake-dragon is visible from the stands, panic and calm are invisible to
judges, and an awakened swimmer at full Fathom could in theory never surface at all. The
15m rule is the one piece of real law that caps them: it is the surface world's only
leash on the deep world, re-litigated every single race. Kai's turns are the show's
tension valve for exactly this reason.*

### Race formats & distances

Individual events: 50/100/200 free are sprint-to-mid-distance; 400/800/1500 free are
distance (both 800 and 1500 free are now standard Olympic events for both men and women as
of the Tokyo cycle); 100/200 back, breast, and fly; 200 and 400 individual medley (IM).
Open water: typically 5k/10k/25k marathon-swim distances, swum in open bodies of water with
a pack-and-navigation dynamic pool swimming doesn't have. Relays: 4×100 and 4×200 freestyle,
4×100 medley.

### How DQs actually happen

Common, real disqualifications: a false start; an illegal turn (no two-hand touch in
breast/fly, or an early one-hand touch); an illegal stroke cycle (alternating kick in
breaststroke, a non-simultaneous breaststroke arm pull, kicking with a scissor/flutter kick
in fly or breast); not touching the wall at the finish; surfacing past 15m or performing
an extra underwater cycle in breaststroke; walking on the pool bottom; pulling on the lane
rope for advantage; a relay swimmer leaving the block before the incoming teammate touches
the wall (an "early takeoff," judged electronically by the starting-block sensor plate);
and unsportsmanlike delay of the race.
*Dramatic use: DQ is the show's built-in "the deep world can't be seen by the judges"
irony generator — an awakened swimmer can win a race by a mile and still be disqualified
for something as mundane as a shoulder angle at the wall. Power doesn't buy immunity from
the rulebook, which keeps surface sport meaningful (elements §6's hard tier gaps).*

### IM order and relay mechanics

Individual medley swims one swimmer through all four strokes in the fixed order
**butterfly → backstroke → breaststroke → freestyle**. The medley *relay*, swum by four
different swimmers, uses a different fixed order —
**backstroke → breaststroke → butterfly → freestyle** — because backstroke has to start
first (it starts in the water, off a backstroke start, and can't take over mid-relay from
a diving start the way the other three can).
*Dramatic use: House rosters map to IM legs in training-arc dialogue — "who swims your
fly leg" is a real question about who your team trusts to hold a lead undulating through a
current-clash, and it's used to characterize House hierarchies without exposition.*

### Sprinter vs. distance swimmer

Real physiological and training divergence: sprinters (50/100) carry more muscle mass,
more fast-twitch (Type IIx/IIa) fiber recruitment, train the ATP-PC and glycolytic
(anaerobic) systems with short, maximal-effort work and full recovery, and race almost
entirely without pacing — it's closer to a dive that happens to include arm strokes.
Distance swimmers (800/1500, open water) are leaner, carry a higher proportion of
slow-twitch (Type I) fiber, train enormous aerobic volume, and race on pacing discipline
and stroke efficiency (distance per stroke) more than raw power.
*Dramatic use: this is the in-world justification for Ren (explosive sprint freestyle,
House-bred power) vs. Kai (who becomes a "deep-and-vertical specialist" — turns and
underwater are a distance-swimmer's economy of motion married to a sprinter's underwater
burst). Bo's mass and Luna's compact diver frame are drawn from the same real body-type
science (bible §11's body-diversity mandate).*

## 2. Freediving physiology, correctly

Everything in this section is real human physiology up to the marked seam. This is the
show's Baki-style anatomy lecture, and it should be taught on-screen exactly as written
here before any mythic extension is shown.

**The mammalian dive reflex.** Cold water contacting the face (specifically the area
served by the trigeminal nerve — forehead, cheeks, area around the nose) triggers an
involuntary cascade: heart rate drops (bradycardia), blood vessels in the limbs
constrict (peripheral vasoconstriction), and blood pressure in the core rises. It's
strongest with cold water on the face, weaker with warm water, weaker still if only the
body (not the face) is submerged. Every air-breathing diving mammal has a stronger
version of the same reflex; humans have a real, measurable, trainable one.
**REAL — this is the whole reflex. The show's version at Sunlit tier is this reflex,
named and dramatized, nothing more.**

**Bradycardia and the "still heart."** Trained freedivers can drop heart rate
substantially below resting during a breath-hold — well-documented, real, and it
*deepens* with practice and calm (panic spikes heart rate and burns oxygen faster,
which is the real mechanism behind "panic is the true enemy," bible §5). Elite
competitive freedivers train this deliberately; it is not a special gift, it is a trained
skill available to any calm, practiced person.
**REAL, and real that it's trainable — this is Bo's whole arc in one sentence.**

**Blood shift and spleen contraction.** At depth, rising ambient pressure would crush an
air-filled lung if the body didn't compensate; blood plasma shifts into the thoracic
cavity and chest blood vessels engorge, cushioning the lungs from the outside and letting
them safely compress well past what "empty lung at the surface" would suggest. Separately,
the spleen — a reservoir of concentrated red blood cells — contracts during a breath-hold
and releases those extra red cells into circulation, measurably boosting oxygen-carrying
capacity for the duration of the dive. This is documented in trained freedivers and, more
strikingly, in the Bajau people of Southeast Asia, whose spleens are notably enlarged from
generations of diving, giving them a real, inherited edge at exactly the kind of
depth-diving their culture practices.
**REAL. This is the single best real-world precedent for the show's "inherited gift" —
the Bajau's spleen size is genuine, documented, heritable physiology that boosts breath-hold
diving capacity. It is the closest thing in nature to a real "Current," which is exactly
why it should be the anatomy lecture that precedes the Current reveal, not follows it.**

**CO₂ vs. O₂ drive — why you feel the urge to breathe, and what actually kills you.**
The urge to breathe is driven overwhelmingly by rising carbon dioxide (and the resulting
drop in blood pH), sensed by chemoreceptors — **not** by falling oxygen. This is the
single most misunderstood fact in every dive scene ever filmed, and it's the physiological
hinge the Water's Rules (§4) turn on: a person can suppress or delay the CO₂
signal (by hyperventilating beforehand, which blows off CO₂ without adding meaningfully
more oxygen to the blood) and keep swimming calmly past the point where oxygen has
dropped low enough to cause loss of consciousness — with no urgent warning signal at all,
because the thing that would have warned them (CO₂) was artificially suppressed. What
kills is hypoxia (oxygen too low to keep the brain conscious), not the discomfort of CO₂
buildup — the discomfort is the alarm; removing the alarm doesn't remove the fire.
**REAL. Load-bearing for the whole show's safety spine — see §4.**

**Shallow-water blackout.** A sudden loss of consciousness from hypoxia, with no warning,
most dangerous on the *ascent*: as a diver rises, ambient pressure drops, and so does the
partial pressure of oxygen in the lungs (and thus what's available to the brain) —
sometimes falling below the threshold for consciousness in the last few meters, even
though the diver "felt fine" seconds before. It is a real, well-documented cause of death
in competitive and recreational breath-hold diving, virtually always preceded by
hyperventilation and/or diving alone.
**REAL — the show's central safety set-piece (§4).**

**Equalization.** Descending divers must actively equalize pressure in the middle ear
(and sinuses) as ambient pressure rises, or risk barotrauma; techniques range from a
simple Valsalva maneuver (closing the nose and gently pressurizing) to the Frenzel
technique (using tongue/throat muscles to push air into the ear without using lung
pressure), which trained deep freedivers rely on because Valsalva stops working well below
the depths at which it's needed.
**REAL — a genuine skill gate, good training-arc material.**

**Static vs. dynamic apnea.** Static apnea (STA) is a breath-hold done face-down,
motionless, in a pool — a pure test of physiology and calm. Dynamic apnea is a breath-hold
done swimming distance underwater, with fins (DYN) or without (DNF) — a test of technique
and efficiency as much as breath-hold capacity.
**REAL — direct real-world analog to the show's "Sunlit tier: read pool currents"
training.**

**Real-world record context (approximate, stated conservatively on purpose).** Elite
competitive static apnea breath-holds, under competition rules (no hyperventilation, no
supplemental oxygen), sit roughly in the neighborhood of ten minutes for the very best
trained humans alive — an extraordinary, years-of-training outlier, not a typical
"advanced amateur" number. Depth records in the deepest unassisted disciplines run roughly
in the hundred-meter range; in the most extreme assisted discipline (a weighted sled down,
inflatable lift back up), roughly twice that. These are world-record, career-culminating
feats for the tiny handful of people who hold them, not baseline competitive ability.

### The seam, marked explicitly

**REAL up to here:** the mammalian dive reflex, bradycardia, blood shift, spleen
contraction, CO₂/O₂ drive, shallow-water blackout, equalization, static/dynamic apnea, and
roughly ten-minute/hundred-meter-plus elite human records, achieved only by full-time
specialist athletes.

**MYTHIC beyond here:** the canon feat table (elements §6) puts *Sunlit* (awakened
novices — "most club swimmers cap here") at a 4-minute static hold and 20m depth-sovereign,
which lines up honestly with a well-trained recreational freediver after real CO₂/O₂-table
work — this tier can be played as barely-elevated reality. *Twilight* (8 minutes, 200m) is
already at or past the outer edge of real elite human achievement — this is the line where
Current, not Breath, has to be doing real work, even though the bible frames Twilight as a
common Fathom Games entry point. *Midnight* (20 minutes) and beyond have no real-world
referent at all and should be played, and shot, as unambiguously supernatural — the wake
dragons made visible (elements §2) are the show's own admission that the physiology has
handed off to myth. Write the transition, don't hide it: the moment a training-arc
character crosses from "this is extraordinary but human" into "this is not human anymore"
is dramatic gold, not a continuity problem to paper over.

## 3. Training methodology

Real, citable structures, each mapped to the show's named Breath training (bible §5:
dive reflex, "still heart" bradycardia, blood shift, spleen contraction, CO₂ tolerance —
called **breath gates** below for writers'-room shorthand).

**Periodization.** Real swim training is built in blocks: a base/aerobic phase (high
volume, moderate intensity, builds the engine), a build phase (volume drops slightly,
intensity and race-pace work rise), a peak/taper phase (volume drops sharply, intensity
stays high, the body arrives at the meet rested but sharp), then competition, then active
recovery before the next cycle.
*Maps to: a full training arc (bible §8's Arc 3, "Twilight Trials") should visibly move
through base → build → taper on screen — the Fathom chart's number should move fastest in
build, and *stop* rising in taper, which is itself a real, correct, and dramatically
useful beat (rest is training, not its absence).*

**CO₂/O₂ tables for apnea, and the safety rules around them.** Two real, opposite
training tools: a **CO₂ table** holds breath-hold time constant while progressively
shortening rest between reps, teaching the body to tolerate rising CO₂ without panicking
(the "still heart" gate). An **O₂ table** holds rest constant while progressively
lengthening the hold, teaching efficient oxygen use (the CO₂-tolerance/blood-shift gates
together). **Non-negotiable real safety rules that must appear on screen whenever a table
is trained:** always with a trained, attentive buddy who is not also holding their breath
at the same time; never after hyperventilating; always in a controlled, shallow, supervised
environment; and any table session stops immediately at the first sign of samba (involuntary
muscle twitching, a warning sign of hypoxia) or LMC (loss of motor control).
*Maps to: this is the literal in-world curriculum for the "breath gates" — Luna teaching
Kai a CO₂ table is both real freediving methodology and the show's coming-of-age training
montage, and the buddy rule folding into it is where §4's rules live inside the actual
lesson, not just as a poster on the wall.*

**Threshold sets.** Repeated swims at or near lactate threshold pace (the effort level
right at the edge of what the body can clear lactate as fast as it produces it), with
short rest — e.g., a real coach might prescribe a broken set of 10×100m on a tight
interval at threshold pace. This is what builds the ability to hold a fast pace without
blowing up, the core currency of every 200–1500 race.
*Maps to: the "still heart" gate under load — a swimmer's heart-rate overlay staying calm
through a threshold set is the visible, on-screen proof of Breath training paying off, per
the standing promise that every power-up shows its bill (GOAL.md dimension 4).*

**Technique drills, per stroke.** Real, standard drills any coach uses: **catch-up
drill** (freestyle — one hand waits, fully extended, at the front until the other hand
completes its full stroke cycle and "catches up," isolating and exaggerating the catch);
**fingertip drag** (freestyle recovery drill — fingertips trail the surface through
recovery, forcing a high elbow); **sculling** (small figure-eight or in-out hand motions
that teach "feel for the water" — pressure sense on the palm/forearm, done in several arm
positions); **fist drill** (swimming with closed fists, forcing reliance on forearm
pressure rather than hand-paddle surface area — over-corrects the hand-only pull);
**zipper drill** (a butterfly-timing drill, thumb tracing up the side of the body on
recovery). Every stroke has an equivalent library; these four are the most universally
taught.
*Maps to: technique drills are how the show visually distinguishes Breath (trainable by
anyone) from Current (inherited) — a training-arc montage of catch-up drills and sculling
is legible to a non-swimmer audience as "hard work," exactly the Rock Lee promise (bible
§6, Bo).*

**Dryland for swimmers.** Real land training supporting swim performance: rotator-cuff
and scapular-stability work (swimmers' shoulders take enormous cyclical load and are the
sport's most common injury site), core/trunk work (the "core" *is* the connection between
the kick and the pull — a weak core leaks power at the hips), ankle flexibility work
(a stiff ankle measurably costs kick propulsion), and explosive/plyometric work for
starts and turns.
*Maps to: Bo's "hauling nets on his family's fishing boat" origin (bible §6) is,
correctly, exactly the kind of full-body functional strength that produces a strong
dryland base without a single formal dryland session — a nice detail to keep, not
retcon.*

**Taper.** The 1-3 week (real range, varies by event distance and athlete) reduction in
training volume before a major competition, while intensity is kept high in short bursts —
the goal is to arrive rested, with glycogen stores topped up and the neuromuscular system
sharp, without losing race feel. A badly executed taper (too long, too short, or wrong
intensity balance) is a real, common way a swimmer "should have won and didn't," and a
legitimate, drama-ready in-story explanation for an upset that has nothing to do with the
mythic layer at all.
*Maps to: a clean, real-world tool for writing a loss that isn't anyone's fault — useful
for the "mid-race reversal, never wire-to-wire" rule (GOAL.md dimension 4) without
reaching for Current interference every time.*

## 4. The Water's Rules

UNDERTOW's stated ambition (bible §13) is to plausibly *teach kids to swim* and model
water safety as a matter of course, not a lecture. Put simply: **the show never
teaches a kid a trick that could kill them — because we want kids copying Kai the way
they copied Naruto.** This section is the hard list: things
no hero — protagonist, rival, or background swim-club kid — does without on-screen
consequence or correction, ever, across the whole series.

1. **Hyperventilating before an underwater swim.** This is the real-world killer (§2's
   CO₂-suppression mechanism) and deserves the full weight the bible already promises for
   drowning (§9: "nobody drowns casually... shot with documentary respect"). **This gets a
   whole episode beat**, not a line of dialogue: a background or secondary character
   over-breathes before a dive (chasing a personal-best, ignorant or overconfident),
   blacks out with zero warning, and is saved *only* because the buddy system worked. Play
   it exactly like the real thing looks — sudden, silent, no struggle, no scream — because
   that accuracy is the entire public-service value of the scene. It should read as more
   frightening than any Current battle in the show, because it is real.
2. **Swimming alone.** No named character trains open water, does an apnea session, or
   free-dives a ruin solo without the show treating it as a visible lapse — either
   corrected by a mentor before it happens, or shown going wrong because it happened.
3. **Breath-hold training in water without a buddy.** Structural, not situational: every
   CO₂/O₂ table, every static-apnea rep, every depth-training session on screen has a
   second person present, watching, not also holding their own breath at the same moment.
4. **Diving into unknown water.** No character enters water of unknown depth, current, or
   hazard without a beat acknowledging the check — reading conditions, confirming depth,
   asking someone who knows the spot — even in a hurry. This one's an easy, cheap,
   constant background detail (a single line, a single glance) that costs nothing and
   compounds across 24 episodes into a real safety habit modeled on screen.

### The buddy system, written into the power system itself

This is the masterstroke of the Water's Rules, and it's already canon (bible §9): Deep
trials — the Fathom Games' relic-retrieval and buddy-rescue gauntlets — are **mechanically
uncrossable alone**. This isn't a rule characters choose to follow; it's a rule the plot
enforces the same way gravity does. A solo swimmer physically cannot clear certain trials,
full stop, regardless of Fathom rank. That single design choice does more real-world
safety work than a hundred lines of dialogue, because it makes "always dive with a buddy"
load-bearing *plot logic* instead of an add-on moral — exactly the kind of "sport truth
married to story mechanics" this whole dimension exists to protect (GOAL.md dimension 6's
bar: buddy protocols baked into the power system, not bolted on).

**Frame for the writers' room:** UNDERTOW should quietly be the show real swim coaches and
learn-to-swim programs recommend to students — every dangerous shortcut a young swimmer
might imitate from a lesser show should instead be the thing UNDERTOW visibly corrects.

## 5. Race-writing cheat sheet

For the writers' room — what actually decides a race at each distance, so every scripted
result is winnable and losable for real reasons, without reaching for Current interference
as a crutch every time.

**50 (free/back/breast/fly).** Decided almost entirely by the start and the underwater.
Reaction time, streamline, and the legal 15m underwater phase (§1) can be the whole race;
there is close to no room to recover from a bad start and very little room for a
mid-race reversal that isn't "won or lost off the block." Write 50s as duels of
stillness, not pacing — the bible's own framing (§9.1) is already exactly correct for this
distance and this distance only.

**100.** The signature "die in the last 15 meters" race. The front 50 is swum on a mix of
aerobic and anaerobic capacity; by the final turn, lactate has accumulated enough that the
last 15m is a pure fight to keep technique from collapsing under fatigue — this is where
races are actually won, not the first 50. A negative or even split (second 50 as fast as
or faster than the first) is the sign of a swimmer who paced correctly; a swimmer who goes
out too fast and visibly comes apart in the last 15m is a completely real, coach-legible
way to write a heartbreak loss.

**200.** The classic "sprinter's worst nightmare, distance swimmer's home turf." Real
pacing wisdom: the third 50 is where 200s are actually decided — it's far enough from the
adrenaline of the start and close enough to the finish that pure pacing discipline (not
raw speed) separates the field. A sprinter-built character visibly struggling on the third
50 of a 200 is real, not a plot contrivance.

**400.** Almost entirely an aerobic-capacity and pacing event; elite swimmers frequently
negative-split it (second half faster than the first) as deliberate strategy, not a fluke.
A character who goes out honest and reels in a field that went out too hot is a completely
real reversal.

**1500 (and 800).** The purest aerobic-engine and stroke-efficiency event in the pool;
distance-per-stroke and the ability to hold technique for dozens of consecutive lengths
matter more than any single burst of speed. Pack dynamics (drafting off another swimmer's
wake, even in adjacent lanes) are real and legal in a way that surprises non-swimmers —
useful for a mid-race tactical beat that doesn't require a rules violation.

**Open water (5/10/25k).** A different sport in the same body. Real deciding factors:
**drafting** (swimming in another competitor's slipstream measurably reduces effort, and
packs form and break for exactly this reason); **positioning and contact** (mass starts
are physically rough — real races have real collisions, kicked goggles, swum-over
swimmers); **sighting/navigation** (lifting the head periodically to check course, at a
real cost to time and rhythm — getting this wrong genuinely loses real races); and
**clean water** (deliberately swimming a slightly longer line to get out of the churn and
wake of the pack, trading distance for efficiency) — a real, correct, and visually
excellent open-water beat for the show's ocean arcs. Reversals in open water believably
happen from pack tactics and course reading, not just raw speed, which is exactly the
"win for real reasons" the show needs from its Meridian open-water events.

**General mid-race psychology, usable everywhere:** races are won by the swimmer who
suffers *correctly paced* suffering, not the least suffering — real racing always hurts,
and the tell of a good swimmer is composure inside the pain, not its absence. This is the
literal, non-mythic version of the bible's mettle thesis ("can you stay yourself in it?")
and should be shot as the emotional truth under every A-plot race even before Current
enters the picture.

## 6. Drawing it right — body line, and the tells that give a show away

Sections 1 to 5 were written for a writer. This one is written for an artist, and it
exists because the codex being correct is worthless if the correctness never reaches a
frame. Every error below is one a swimmer spots in the first second, and once they spot
one they stop believing the rest of the show.

### The head rule

**The head is the tell.** More than the arms, more than the kick. A raised head drops the
hips, angles the body nose-up, and multiplies drag — so a swimmer with their head up is,
without exception, being drawn as a beginner.

| Context | Head |
|---|---|
| **Freestyle** | Neutral. Eyes down, or down-and-slightly-forward. The waterline crosses the crown. To breathe the head **rotates with the body roll** — it does not lift. One goggle stays in the water, in the bow-wave trough beside the head. |
| **Butterfly** | Eyes down, chin barely clearing to breathe, head back down before the hands enter. |
| **Backstroke** | Absolutely still. Eyes up, ears submerged, waterline across the cheeks. |
| **Breaststroke** | Rises and falls with the pull, always in line with the spine. Never craned. |
| **Streamline** | Squeezed between the biceps. Eyes down. |
| **Freediving / dynamic apnea** | The most neutral of all — chin slightly tucked, eyes down. Looking ahead is the single most expensive drag error in the sport. |

### There is no arm recovery underwater

Over-the-water recovery exists for exactly one reason: air is roughly eight hundred times
less dense than water. Swinging an arm forward through *water* costs more than it returns,
so nobody does it, ever. **A freestyle high-elbow recovery drawn underwater is not a
stylisation — it is a thing that does not happen.**

Underwater, a swimmer is doing one of precisely three things:

1. **Streamline plus dolphin kick** — off the start and every turn, in all four strokes.
   Arms locked overhead, hands stacked, undulation running from the chest through pointed
   toes. This is faster than any surface stroke, which is why the 15m rule exists.
2. **The breaststroke pullout** — one pull all the way to the thighs, hands recovered
   *close to the body* under the chest, one dolphin kick and one breaststroke kick.
3. **Dynamic apnea** — glide-dominant, arms in streamline or at the sides, the least
   movement that will still carry you. This is what UNDERTOW's freedivers do.

If a shot needs arms doing something underwater, it is one of those three, or the
character is not swimming — they are sinking, reaching, or drowning, which are all legitimate
and all look completely different.

### How to check a streamline in one glance

The streamline is the most-drawn position in a swimming show and the easiest to get
subtly wrong. Two arms reaching forward with a gap between the hands is not a streamline,
it is a "superman glide" — it looks close, and every swimmer reads it as wrong.

There is a one-second test, and it is also the most useful thing to put in a brief:

> **In a true side profile, a correct streamline shows only ONE arm and ONE hand.**

The far arm is directly behind the near one and the far hand is directly behind the near
hand, so they occlude completely. The body tapers to a single flat blade. If a profile
shot shows two hands side by side, or a V of water between the arms, the position is
wrong no matter how good the rest of the drawing is.

The corollary is worth stating too: the biceps squeeze hard enough that the ears are
covered and the head is buried between the upper arms. Only the jaw, the goggles and the
underside of the face should show beneath the arm. If the whole head is visible with a
clear gap on both sides, the arms are not locked.

### Kit, and what is actually legal

- **Surface kit, men:** textile **jammers**, waist to above the knee. No sleeves. Full-body
  and non-textile suits have been banned since 2010, so a sleeved bodysuit in a lane-lined
  competition pool is not race kit and reads as a costume error to anyone who swims.
- **Surface kit, women:** textile one-piece, shoulders to above the knee at most.
- **Deep kit** — the Houses' ancestral second-skins — is full coverage by design. That is
  *the deep*. It does not belong in a competition pool, and the contrast between the two
  is a storytelling asset, not an inconsistency to smooth over.

### Smaller tells, in the order they get noticed

- **Toes pointed.** A flexed foot is a brake. Plantarflexed ankles in every kicking shot.
- **Fingers slightly apart.** A small gap is genuinely faster than a sealed paddle — but
  slightly, not splayed.
- **Bubbles mean exhaling.** A breath-holding swimmer emits almost nothing. A stream of
  bubbles from the mouth is a deliberate choice with meaning in this show, never a default
  decoration.
- **Lane lines float at the surface.** A swimmer a metre down should have them *above* them,
  not beside them at eye level.
- **The catch is high-elbow.** The forearm goes vertical early (EVF) — the elbow stays high
  and forward while the hand presses back. A straight-arm windmill is a different, older,
  slower stroke.

## 7. Glossary

Real term — one-line definition — *in-world slang, where the show has (or should coin)
one.*

1. **Streamline** — the tightest, most hydrodynamic body position (arms extended overhead,
   biceps squeezing ears, hands stacked) used off every start and turn. *"Holding the
   line."*
2. **Catch** — the moment early in the underwater pull where the hand/forearm sets against
   still water and stops slipping — the true start of propulsion.
3. **EVF (Early Vertical Forearm)** — the modern high-elbow catch technique; the forearm
   goes vertical early in the pull instead of dragging flat.
4. **Breakout** — the transition from underwater streamline into the first surface stroke
   after a start or turn.
5. **Pace clock** — the large, sweeping clock on deck swimmers use to time intervals and
   sets, read at a glance mid-stroke.
6. **Drafting** — swimming in the reduced-resistance wake of another swimmer, legal and
   common in open water, illegal-by-convention (though rarely enforceable) in some pool
   contexts.
7. **Apnea walk** — a freediving discipline: walking a set distance on land while holding
   the breath, used in dryland breath training. *A common "Sunlit gate" drill.*
8. **Packing (glossopharyngeal insufflation, "buccal pumping")** — forcing extra gulps of
   air into already-full lungs using the mouth/throat as a pump. **Dangerous**: it can
   overexpand the lungs and cause barotrauma (including risk of an air embolism), and it's
   banned or discouraged in most competitive static-apnea rulesets for exactly that
   reason — the show should treat it the way it treats hyperventilating: a shortcut with a
   real, on-screen cost if anyone tries it.
9. **Lactate threshold** — the effort intensity at which the body's lactate clearance can
   no longer keep pace with its production; the pace a swimmer can sustain without rapid
   fatigue.
10. **Negative split** — swimming the second half of a race faster than the first; a mark
    of correct pacing.
11. **Positive split** — swimming the second half slower than the first — usually a sign
    of going out too hard.
12. **DPS (Distance Per Stroke)** — how far the body travels per stroke cycle; a core
    efficiency metric, especially for distance swimmers.
13. **Bilateral breathing** — alternating breathing sides in freestyle (e.g., every third
    stroke) rather than always to one side; builds symmetry and race awareness of both
    lanes.
14. **Hypoxic set** — a training set done breathing less often than normal (e.g., every 5,
    7, or 9 strokes) to build CO₂ tolerance and breath control. *Directly trains the
    "still heart" gate.*
15. **Dolphin kick** — the simultaneous, whip-driven two-leg kick used underwater off every
    start/turn and throughout butterfly.
16. **Whip kick** — the breaststroke kick: heels to glutes, feet turned out, snapped around
    and together.
17. **Sculling** — small figure-eight hand motions used to build feel for water pressure;
    a foundational drill across all strokes.
18. **Flip turn (tumble turn)** — the forward-somersault turn used in freestyle and
    backstroke.
19. **Open turn** — the two-hand-touch turn required in breaststroke and butterfly.
20. **False start** — leaving the block before the starting signal; a single-strike DQ
    under current rules.
21. **Relay exchange (takeoff)** — the incoming swimmer's wall touch and the outgoing
    swimmer's block departure, electronically timed; an early takeoff is a DQ.
22. **IM (Individual Medley)** — a single swimmer racing all four strokes in fixed order:
    fly, back, breast, free.
23. **Medley relay** — four swimmers, one stroke each, in fixed order: back, breast, fly,
    free.
24. **Stroke count** — the number of strokes taken per length; tracked as an efficiency and
    pacing metric.
25. **Taper** — the pre-competition reduction in training volume meant to arrive at a meet
    rested and sharp.
26. **VO2 max** — the maximum rate the body can take up and use oxygen during intense
    exercise; a standard (if imperfect) marker of aerobic capacity.
27. **Glide** — the momentary motionless streamline phase in breaststroke (and off every
    push-off), used to let speed carry the body before the next propulsive phase begins.
28. **Recovery (arm)** — the portion of the stroke cycle where the arm returns from the
    finish of the pull to the entry point, over or under water depending on stroke.
29. **Pull buoy** — a foam training float held between the thighs to isolate the arms
    (removes the kick) during drill work.
30. **Paddles** — hand paddles worn in training to overload the pull and sharpen catch
    feedback.
31. **Kickboard** — a foam board held at arm's length to isolate kick training.
32. **Static apnea (STA)** — a breath-hold performed motionless, face-down, testing pure
    physiological limits. *Maps to the show's "Sunlit" static-breath feats.*
33. **Dynamic apnea (DYN/DNF)** — a breath-hold performed swimming for distance
    underwater, with fins (DYN) or without (DNF).
34. **Equalization (Valsalva / Frenzel)** — actively pressurizing the middle ear against
    rising ambient pressure while descending; Frenzel uses tongue/throat muscles and works
    at depths where Valsalva no longer does.
35. **Samba** — involuntary muscle twitching from hypoxia at the end of a breath-hold; a
    hard-stop warning sign for any freediver or safety buddy.
36. **LMC (Loss of Motor Control)** — a more severe hypoxia warning sign than samba,
    immediately preceding blackout; an automatic session-ending signal.
37. **Shallow-water blackout** — sudden loss of consciousness from hypoxia underwater,
    most dangerous on ascent, almost always preceded by hyperventilation and/or diving
    without a buddy. *The show's central safety set-piece (§4).*
38. **Mammalian dive reflex** — the involuntary bradycardia/vasoconstriction response
    triggered by cold water on the face. *In-world: the "First Gate," everyone's Breath
    starting point.*
39. **Blood shift** — plasma shift into the thoracic cavity at depth that protects the
    lungs from pressure-related collapse. *In-world: the "Shift Gate."*
40. **Spleen contraction (splenic autotransfusion)** — the spleen's release of extra red
    blood cells into circulation during a breath-hold, boosting oxygen-carrying capacity;
    the real, documented, heritable precedent (Bajau divers) for the show's inherited-gift
    premise. *In-world: the deepest and most "Current-adjacent" of the Breath gates —
    exactly where the writers' room should let the line between real physiology and
    inherited myth blur on purpose.*
